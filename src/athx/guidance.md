# 3. Training Rules
Here is a quick-fire list of training rules (more detail in **Appendix A**)
- Calculate percentages (E.g. 80% of 1RM) from a current/recent (≤6 weeks) training 1RM.
- The written percentage is the planned *starting* weight.
- RPE and exercise technique are the top priority (over weight).
- Reduce the weight in 2.5-5 kg steps if the weight you’ve chosen means a higher RPE than is prescribed or your form deteriorates.
	- *E.g.* it says do 5 reps at RPE 8 for your Squats. You choose 120 kg but you have to grind the last rep (RPE 10) - you would drop to 115 kg.
- Do not increase above the written weight - the workouts are carefully periodised.
- RPE 8 = two technically sound repetitions remain.
- <span underline="true">**DO NOT**</span> grind or fail repetitions - reduce the weight if this happens.
- Round to the nearest sensible weight you can assemble.
	- *E.g.* if your estimated weight is 121 kg but the smallest plates you have are 2.5 kg - stick with 120 kg.
- Rest 3-5 minutes between main lift sets unless the session says otherwise.
- Accessory movements can be trained a little harder between RPE 8-9. 
- For accessory movements <span underline="true">**only**</span>: progress reps <span underline="true">**before**</span> weight, only adding weight once your sets reach the upper end at the required effort (RPE).
	- *E.g.* for 8-12 reps at RPE 8, you choose 30 kg and do 9/8/7 reps; you would progress the weight to 32.5 kg when you are achieving something like 12/11/10 reps that still feel like RPE 8.
<callout icon="/icons/info-alternate_gray.svg">
	IF percentages confuse you, don’t stress, just use RPE (”Effort” - out of 10) to guide your weights.
</callout>

	For example:
	**Strength** 420 kg vs 440 target = 4.5% deficit
	**Endurance** 5 050 m vs 5 300 target = 4.7% deficit
**Endurance** should be the focus for this athlete, as it has the greatest percentage deficit.

If two qualities tie, calculate each result's percentage deficit from the Strong threshold:
- **Higher-is-better result (Lifts/Endurance)**: `((Strong threshold - result) ÷ Strong threshold) × 100`
	- *E.g. *for a Female Lite athlete:* *`((225 kg - 220 kg) ÷ 225 kg) × 100` = <span underline="true">**2.22% deficit**</span>
- **Lower-is-better time (MetCon/5km)**: `((result in seconds - Strong threshold in seconds) ÷ Strong threshold in seconds) × 100` 
	- *E.g. *for a Female Lite athlete:* *`((970 seconds - 750 seconds) ÷ 750 seconds) × 100` = <span underline="true">**29.33% deficit**</span>
If you compare the above two directly, it’s pretty clear that MetCon is the limiter for this athlete.

# 5. Rearranging/Missing Sessions & Extra Work
The normal order is squat, Zone 2, press plus Run:Row, rest, deadlift plus MetCon, Bonus, rest.  
- This program is designed to work entirely on its own without the need for any additional work but I appreciate may do some extra; just try to keep <span underline="true">**one**</span> of the rest days protected.
- <span underline="true">**Avoid**</span> placing heavy lower-body work immediately after a demanding run or MetCon.
- If combining a lift and conditioning session, lift first.
- Keep at least one recovery day between the deadlift plus MetCon session and another substantial lower-body exposure where possible.
	- *E.g.* if your bonus work is Strength, do the deadlift plus MetCon session on Thursday and rest Friday instead.
- When time is limited, prioritise the most event-specific session and/or your current limiter/weakness.
- Do not push through sessions if you develop abnormal pain or injury. Seek guidance from an appropriate physical therapist.

# 7. Return Week (After Competition)
Use the Return track for the first week after competition to ensure you’re adequately recovered and ready to make progress again. <span underline="true">**DON’T**</span> go straight back onto the Main track! 
- Complete the Day 1 squat option only after a Saturday competition when soreness is no higher than 4/10 and readiness feels at least a 6/10.
- After a Sunday competition, or when soreness/readiness are elevated, use the walking option in the track.
- Run only if impact is pain-free.
- There is <span underline="true">**no limiter session**</span> during Return.
- If readiness, soreness or fatigue worsens, rest completely.
- Join Main after **1 week** on the Return track 
# 8. FAQ
## Can I join midway through the season?
Yes. Follow the steps in “Start Here” and choose the appropriate track.
## When do I switch to Comp Ready?
At Three Weeks Out. Comp Ready replaces Main for the remainder of the countdown to your event.
## What happens during the week after competition?
Complete <span underline="true">**1 week**</span> on the Return track to recover, then rejoin Main or the relevant Comp Ready phase according to the next event date.
## What should I do if I miss or rearrange a session?
If you’re feeling good for it, use your rest days and sensibly adjust the weights for a given day: e.g. if you’re busy on Friday but not Thursday, move the deadlift plus MetCon session to Thursday - you may just need to just reduce the deadlift load by a few kilos here and there if you’re still a little tired from the Weds session. Getting the total work done at 5-10% less is almost <span underline="true">**always**</span> better than missing the work.
## How do I handle back to back events?
**Eight weeks** is the minimum coaching recommendation for a practical window of recovery, a short development block and another taper. **Twelve weeks or more **is preferable when the aim is meaningful progress between events. Honestly, if you’re trying to do more than 2-3 of these a year, don’t expect yourself to progress much. You might think maxxing out looks good for the gram, but your body won’t think it’s clever nor reward you with the progress you want.

# Appendix A. Detailed Training Guidance & Examples
## Workout Details
### Movement Prep & Warm-up Sets
I *will* include a general mobility section for those that prefer to do a little before training but this is *optional*. You <span underline="true">**don’t**</span> need elaborate 30-40 minute warm-ups. If you’re short on time or simply don’t feel like you need the mobility work beforehand, simply performing some lighter sets of your main movements first will suffice. The key with the warm up sets is to *not* spend unnecessary energy on them. 
You <span underline="true">**don’t**</span> need to get “a pump” to be sufficiently warmed up - you just need a few reps at weights that are incrementally larger and move closer to your working weight.
#### Example
Week 1 has the following programmed:
	**Strength & Power**
		**A)** Barbell Back Squat
			Set 1 =  3 reps @ 82.5% 1RM\*<br>Sets 2-5 = 3 reps @ 75% 1RM<br>Rest: 3-4 minutes per set<br>Difficulty: 7-8 RPE
	**Accessory**
		**B)** Bulgarian Split Squat - 3 x 6-8 reps/side
		**C)** Seated/Lying Leg Curl - 3 x 8-12 reps
		**D)** Seated Calf Raise - 3 x 8-12 reps
		**E)** Ab Wheel Rollouts - 3 x 6-12 reps
If I was going to do 150 kg for Set 1, I would perform 2-3 warm up sets for Squats as follows:
	**WU1**: 6 reps x 60 kg<br>**WU2**: 4 reps x 100 kg<br>**WU3**: 2 reps x 130 kg
And perhaps 1 rep x 140 kg.
I *wouldn’t* do any warm ups for the Bulgarian Split Squats as they should already be plenty warm from the Barbell Squats.
	**Leg Curl** (a low complexity machine based movement) - I would do one quick warm up set.<br>**Calf Raises** (as above) - as above<br>**Abs** - I wouldn’t do any (they work all day, because gravity)
Depending on many factors, you may need/want more or less warm-up sets than others and ultimately, how you approach this is up to you. But remember, they exist to *prepare* you for the working sets, nothing more. Don’t waste energy.

## RPE & %1RM
#### **RPE** (Rate of Perceived Exertion)
Relax… It’s nowhere near as complicated as you think. RPE is explained below:
![](/images/athx/guidance-01.png)
If I write **3 sets x 6-8 reps @** **RPE 7-8**, that means that I want you to pick a weight where if you did anywhere between 6-8 reps, it would feel moderate to difficult and like you could have done 2-3 more reps if I held a gun to your head. 
You may choose to use the same weight, and let the RPE accumulate as you do each successive set, for example:
	**Set 1**: 30 kg x 8 reps @ RPE 7<br>**Set 2**: 30 kg x 8 reps @ RPE 8<br>**Set 3**: 30 kg x 7 reps @ RPE 8
Because fatigue is “a thing”, you’ll notice the RPE increased between Set 1 and Set 2, and by Set 3, to stay within the guidance of “RPE 7-8”
Instead, you may choose to use the same weight, and *decrease the reps* to let the RPE stay more stable as the sets go on, for example:
	**Set 1**: 30 kg x 8 reps @ RPE 7<br>**Set 2**: 30 kg x 7 reps @ RPE 7<br>**Set 3**: 30 kg x 6 reps @ RPE 7.5
There is <span underline="true">**no**</span> “better” option. It’s down to personal preference.
Use RPE to choose and adjust the load while staying inside the written repetition range. For accessories, progress repetitions within the range first and add load once every set reaches the upper end at the required effort. For main lifts, reduce the load when the written percentage would breach the RPE or technical cap. Do not add repetitions or increase above the percentage unless that Fitr entry explicitly calls for an autoregulated attempt.
This is a <span underline="true">**skill**</span> and takes time to master if you haven’t done this before, so relax. In 15 years and after coaching *thousands* of people, I can tell you that about 90% of people have 99 problems but “training too hard” ain’t fucking one of them. To date, I know <span underline="true">**one**</span> person who’s *genuinely* overtrained to the point of rhabdo. 
The best instinctive guidance I can give for being “in the right ball park” are these two things: 
	1) If you pick a weight and every set you’re hitting the same number of reps - you are **nowhere **<span underline="true">**near**</span> RPE 10, and more than likely ≤RPE 7.
	2) The point where a weight starts to feel like it’s suddenly pushing back against you more than it should was throughout the rest of the set, is *probably* around RPE 7-8 for *most* movements.
The general rule is that the more “heavy duty” a movement - e.g. a Barbell Back Squat Vs a Bicep Curl - the more of a cost on recovery higher RPE values will have. I.e. doing 10 sets of Barbell Back Squats at RPE 10 can leave you feeling like you need a lie down in a dark room and on the next day like you don’t want to go anywhere near the gym for bullying you, whereas the same scenario of Bicep Curls feels like a good pre-night out pump/buzz.
As such, you will notice that I have continually reminded you <span underline="true">**not**</span> to pump the RPE on the big movements. You might feel macho macho on Week 1, but you won’t feel so clever by Week 4 when you have another 3 to go. No one gives a fuck if “you could do more” in the gym if your lifts come comp day are shit.

#### **%1RM**
percentage of your 1 rep maximum (1RM) for a given lift.
	*E.g.* if my 1RM Back Squat is 170 kg and it says “Sets 2-5 = 3 reps @ 75% 1RM”:
		170 kg x 75% / 100 = <span underline="true">**127.5 kg**</span>
If you don’t have a recent max for a given lift, you can also estimate it [here](https://strengthlevel.com/one-rep-max-calculator) by punching in the weight and reps. 
**Note**: this is going to assume this is an RPE 10 effort, so if you’re trying to estimate from a set that wasn’t “all out” - estimate how many more you could have done and *add* that to the total reps.

### Rest Between Sets
Rest periods will be prescribed when I think a specific rest period is important, but most of the time, I would go by **1-2 minutes** in between *smaller*, *less* complex movements (e.g. Bicep Curls, Lateral Raises, Cable Lat Pulldown etc.) and **3+ minutes** for *bigger*, more *taxing* movements (e.g. Barbell Back Squat, Deadlift, Barbell Bench Press). 
The same goes for **supersets:** if I have programmed a superset (e.g. exercise A1 & A2), I do <span underline="true">**not**</span> want you to between them like someone’s holding a gun to your head… Simply do exercise 1, move to the next exercise at a comfortable pace - resting briefly as needed -, do exercise 2, rest as needed before starting the next superset.
If you have plenty of time, you can rest as long as you’d like, the only drawback is that your session will take longer; so if you need to be more efficient, time your rest. I’d rather see someone focus on quality over quantity, so I would generally advise resting longer (especially for things like **strength** movements) than rush through a bunch of low quality sets. 
### Modifications & Substitutions
I wrote this program with practicality and simplicity in mind, such that most people in most gyms should be able to do the movements prescribed without issue. However, I don’t expect everyone doing the program to be universal in capabilities or have access to the same equipment. If you have questions about modifications or substitutions, just drop a message in the chat. Be organised - the chat isn’t monitored 24/7 so if you foresee an issue, don’t leave it to in the middle of your session… 
Some may find that a certain lifting percentage is a little too high or low for a given RPE or vice versa, so if you need to adjust or modify some of the variables like loading or reps, that’s OK. The main thing is that we’re working as close to competition conditions as possible, but that does <span underline="true">**not**</span> mean that if you don’t feel confident enough with say a 3 rep set weight for Squats (pretty damn heavy) to mirror the 3RM required in competition, that if you decided to use a 5 rep weight that it wouldn’t contribute to performance. 
# Balancing Strength & Conditioning
Some of you might be wondering how to optimise the combination of two seemingly opposite disciplines which I’ll start by addressing a common thought that lives at the back of everyone’s minds: 
> Is it possible to progress **maximally** in *both* strength and conditioning at the **same time**?
The simple answer is: <span underline="true">**no**</span>.
But that’s not what Hybrid training is about… 
The whole *point* is that it is sufficiently difficult that one has to hedge their bets tactically and carefully balance their outputs. If you *could* maximise both without sacrifice, you’d have Hafþór Björnsson running sub 2 hour marathons and Eliud Kipchoge deadlifting 3+ times his bodyweight…
There *are* freaks of nature - e.g. Ronnie Coleman or Tia-Clair Toomey - that can excel at multiple disciplines better than others but that’s just it - they’re <span underline="true">**not**</span> normal and like it or not, those genetic abnormalities that provide us with such doe-eyed, “edge of your seat” excitement would beat you every day of the week if you followed the same program. So don’t base *your* ceiling or capacity on someone else’s as some things simply *won’t* be accessible for you. 
Anywho, the literature term for this physiological *conflict* between strength vs speed or animal vs athletic is called the “**Interference Effect**” or “**Concurrent Training**” which represent the negative impact of one discipline and it’s adaptation stimulus on the other, and the combination of resistance and endurance training in the same program respectively. Fortunately, it has been studied *at length.* I won’t bore you with the details and history but instead, I’ll provide you with a simple ruleset: 
- **Prioritise what matters most and do it when freshest**. *E.g. Strict Press before conditioning*.
- **Separate hard endurance and lifting by 6+ hours** where possible. *E.g. Squats AM, Intervals PM; if Squats are most important.*
- **If combined**, do the priority discipline first. *E.g. Strict Press before conditioning; if Strict Press is most important.*
- **Keep most aerobic work easy** so it does not compromise key sessions. *E.g. Zone 2 rather than another threshold session.*
- **Manage total lower-body fatigue**, not just session count. *Hard running, Squats and MetCons all count (i.e. additional work on top of this program).*
- **Fuel the workload**, *particularly with adequate carbohydrate around harder sessions*.
- **If performance repeatedly drops, reduce the least important fatigue first**. *Usually  accessory volume.*
**Why am I telling you all of this?** Well, you will notice that the order of the sessions is automatically considerate of minimising the impact of this *however*, there will come a week where you’ve been asked to stay late at work, you slept badly because of a newborn (or the heatwave in the UK, at the time of writing), your boiler broke and you needed to be home for the gas engineer - whatever. There will be times where your schedule needs to change, where this will provide you with the necessary knowledge and confidence to independently re-shuffle your week. 
If shit well and truly hits the fan, the *obvious* solution is <span underline="true">**not**</span> to abandon the whole week, but to try your best to prioritise your **weaknesses**. 
And when it comes to re-shuffling your workouts, there are “perfect” ways to go about it, but there are a few key mistakes to avoid:
**Mistake #1**: Doing a heavy lift immediately after (within a few hours) of a high intensity run. Either lift first (if combining sessions) or separate the sessions by several hours. The exception to this rule would be that’s it fine to lift heavy after a low volume sprint session (ie short sprints / acceleration work), But I’d avoid something like heavy squats right after a 10x800m workout or tempo run.
**Mistake #2:** Doing speed work while sore. Doing easy runs on sore legs is not a problem, but I’d avoid doing speed work or MetCon work if your leg soreness is above a 3/10. If this is a consistent problem, consider rearranging your workouts so that speed work falls on a day that your legs feel a little better, or reducing volume (e.g. dropping a set) on the exercises causing the most soreness. While running on tired legs is not that big of a problem, routinely running on legs that are very sore and significantly holding back your run performance isn’t worth it.
**Best practice: **isolate the most demanding high intensity run session of your week, and prioritise it somewhere in your calendar where you know you’ll feel pretty good and have your best chance at a good workout. Prioritising feeling fresh for one quality run workout per week is helpful, even if it’s not practical to feel great for all of them. Generally I’d prioritise the session that is the **most specific** to your goal even, while allowing yourself to be a bit more fatigued for general workouts and easy sessions.
